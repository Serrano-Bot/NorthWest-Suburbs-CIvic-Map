Printing: set the title, paper size, and orientation above, then use "Print current view." The printed sheet gets its own header with a name/date line and an auto-built legend of whatever layers are turned on.
Township: the oldest layer of local government here, set up when Illinois was first surveyed in the 1800s. Its jobs are road and bridge upkeep outside city limits, property assessment, and General Assistance aid for residents in need — run by an elected supervisor and trustees. Boundaries are essentially permanent. For example: plowing snow on an unincorporated street, running a food pantry or emergency rent assistance program, assessing your home's value for the tax bill, and maintaining a bridge outside any city's limits.
City / village: a self-governing municipality created when residents voted to incorporate. Handles the most visible day-to-day services — police, fire, water/sewer, local streets, parks, zoning — run by an elected mayor/president and council. Boundaries can grow over time through annexation as nearby landowners petition to join. For example: a 911 police response, issuing a building permit for a home addition, deciding the local speed limit, and approving (or denying) a zoning variance for a new store.
Neighboring school district: an independent unit of government — separate from any city or township — whose only job is running public schools: hiring staff, setting curriculum, and levying the property tax that pays for it. Governed by an elected school board. Boundaries rarely change, though a shrinking district can be dissolved into a neighbor. For example: setting the school calendar and snow days, hiring a new principal, approving a new curriculum, and negotiating the teachers' union contract.
HSD 211: runs the five public high schools here (grades 9–12 only — elementary/middle schools belong to separate districts like D54), governed by an elected board from its administration center in Palatine. Attendance-boundary toggle uses a 2015–16 federal survey, the best public approximation available — for the current official version, see D211_official_boundary_map.pdf. For example: setting graduation requirements, adding a new AP course at Schaumburg HS, hiring the superintendent, and passing the district's annual budget.
D54: Schaumburg CCSD 54 runs the elementary/middle schools feeding into HSD 211 — the largest elementary district in Illinois by enrollment. Same governance structure as HSD 211, and the same 2015–16 survey caveat applies to its attendance-zone toggle. For example: deciding which elementary school your street is zoned for, hiring elementary teachers, approving all-day kindergarten, and building a new school when a neighborhood grows.
Census tract: drawn by the Census Bureau purely for counting and statistics (~4,000 people each) — no elected officials, no services, no taxes. Redrawn every 10 years after each census. For example: calculating a neighborhood's poverty rate, deciding eligibility for federal community-development grants, and helping a school district or retailer figure out where population is growing.
County: the layer of government above townships and cities. Runs courts, the sheriff and jail, tax assessment/collection, elections, and public health — governed by an elected county board. Boundaries were fixed in the 1800s and essentially never change. For example: issuing marriage licenses, running the county jail, mailing your property tax bill, and operating public health clinics that give vaccines.
County Commissioner district: the district that elects one of the 17 members of the Cook County Board of Commissioners, the county's actual legislature — it passes the county budget and county ordinances. Redrawn once a decade after each census. For example: voting on a county gas or tobacco tax, passing a county minimum-wage ordinance, and deciding funding for the county forest preserves.
Board of Review district: elects one of three commissioners who hear property-tax assessment appeals countywide — a narrow, specific job distinct from the County Board. Redrawn each decade like other county districts. For example: lowering a homeowner's assessed value after an appeal, ruling on a commercial property's tax dispute, and granting a senior citizen's tax exemption.
Judicial subcircuit: elects judges to the Cook County Circuit Court for that specific area, rather than countywide — created in the 1990s so judges could be elected by nearby voters instead of county-wide slating. Redrawn periodically by the state legislature. For example: a judge in this subcircuit might preside over a local divorce/custody case, a landlord-tenant dispute, a traffic case, or a criminal trial for something that happened nearby.
U.S. Congressional district: elects one member to the U.S. House of Representatives. Redrawn every 10 years by the Illinois legislature after each census — historically one of the more contentious redistricting fights in the state. For example: voting on federal spending bills, voting to authorize military action, and voting on national policy like Social Security or immigration law.
State Senate / State House district: elects members of the Illinois General Assembly — the state's actual lawmaking body, which passes state law, the state budget, and sets policy for schools, Medicaid, and more. Also redrawn every 10 years post-census. For example: setting the state minimum wage, funding public schools statewide, passing state gun laws, and legalizing (or banning) something like recreational marijuana.
Not included: Community College, Fire Protection, Public Library, Park, and Water/Sanitary districts (also shown in Schaumburg Township's official precinct package) — no current, reliably public live-data source was found for these, so they were left out rather than guessed.
Voting precinct: the smallest unit of election administration — each has one polling place and its own local ballot. Precincts don't govern anything; they exist purely to organize voting. The county clerk redraws lines periodically, usually after redistricting following each census. For example: deciding which building you vote in, which exact races appear on your ballot, and how election results get counted and reported.
Boundary data: U.S. Census Bureau TIGERweb (live). Basemaps: Stadia Maps / OpenStreetMap contributors.
